1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
===============================================================================

ArcVanta AI is a mobile basketball intelligence and coaching platform that uses
the phone camera to analyze shooting, movement, ball trajectory, player
mechanics, consistency, and training progress in real time.

The experience shown in the supplied reference video provides the starting
concept:

- Detect the player.
- Draw body landmarks.
- Detect the basketball and hoop.
- trace the ball path.
- Count made shots and total attempts.

ArcVanta AI will expand this basic concept into a complete commercial product
for individual players, parents, trainers, coaches, teams, and basketball
facilities. It will combine real-time on-device computer vision with optional
cloud analysis, personalized coaching, advanced biomechanics, training plans,
automatic highlights, and long-term performance intelligence.

The application will be developed once with a shared Flutter product layer for
iOS and Android. Performance-critical camera and AI workloads will use native
iOS and Android integrations.

The core computer-vision architecture will use:

- MediaPipe Pose for detailed full-body landmarks.
- MediaPipe Hands for wrist, palm, finger, and release-hand analysis.
- A custom RTMDet-Tiny model for basketball, hoop, rim, net, player, and court
  object detection.
- ByteTrack for persistent object identities across frames.
- Kalman filtering for motion prediction and recovery from short occlusions.
- OpenCV for geometry, trajectory reconstruction, calibration, shot-event
  calculations, and visual overlays.
- A basketball-trained RTMPose model as an advanced pose engine for fast
  motion, multiple players, difficult angles, and high-performance analysis.

The system must not depend on a generative AI model to calculate objective
metrics. Shot counts, angles, release timing, trajectory, balance, and scoring
must come from deterministic computer-vision and biomechanics pipelines.
Generative AI may explain verified results in natural language, but it must
never invent measurements.

===============================================================================
2. PRODUCT VISION
===============================================================================

Create the most useful camera-based basketball development system available on
a normal phone, without requiring a dedicated tracking camera, wearable, smart
ball, or sensor installation.

The product must:

- Work in indoor and outdoor courts.
- Work with a single phone and standard tripod.
- Provide useful live feedback without interrupting the workout.
- Explain what the player should change and why.
- Measure improvement over days, weeks, months, and seasons.
- Support both individual training and coach-managed athletes.
- Process sensitive video privately whenever possible.
- Continue functioning when internet access is unavailable.
- Produce trustworthy metrics with visible confidence indicators.
- Avoid advertisements that interrupt training.

The strategic advantage is not a single AI model. The advantage will come from
combining:

- Basketball-specific training data.
- Multiple specialized computer-vision models.
- A temporal shot-understanding engine.
- Accurate calibration and confidence management.
- A coaching system grounded in measured data.
- High-quality live graphics.
- A growing history of each player's mechanics and performance.

3. PRODUCT GOALS
===============================================================================

3.1 Primary goals

- Detect, track, and classify basketball shots automatically.
- Count makes, misses, attempts, streaks, and shooting locations.
- Analyze complete shooting mechanics from preparation through landing.
- Track the ball from possession through release, flight, rim interaction, and
  result.
- Deliver immediate, understandable coaching feedback.
- Create personalized drills based on actual weaknesses.
- Provide professional session reports and progress trends.
- Operate smoothly on supported iOS and Android devices.

3.2 Commercial goals

- Launch a subscription product without forced advertising.
- Serve players, parents, independent trainers, teams, academies, and
  facilities.
- Create defensible intellectual property through basketball datasets,
  calibration logic, temporal-event models, and coaching intelligence.
- Support individual, coach, and organization plans.
- Reduce cloud inference costs by processing live sessions on the device.

3.3 Non-goals for the first public release

- Replacing certified medical, rehabilitation, or injury-diagnosis services.
- Claiming laboratory-grade 3D biomechanics from one uncalibrated camera.
- Official referee decision-making.
- Guaranteed recruiting, scholarship, or professional performance outcomes.
- Full five-on-five tactical analysis from a single phone.
- Support for desktop, web-camera, smart glasses, or game consoles.

5. CORE USER JOURNEYS
===============================================================================

5.1 First-time setup

1. Install the application.
2. Select player, parent, trainer, or coach account type.
3. Create an account or continue in limited offline guest mode.
4. Complete privacy, camera, microphone, storage, and analytics consent.
5. Create the player profile, dominant hand, position, age band, height, skill
   level, and goals.
6. View a short camera-placement guide.
7. Place the phone on a tripod and complete automatic court calibration.
8. Run a three-shot calibration test.
9. Receive a camera-quality and confidence confirmation.

5.2 Live shooting session

1. Select a drill or Quick Session.
2. Choose front, side, rear, or diagonal camera placement.
3. The app confirms player, ball, hoop, and floor visibility.
4. Start a countdown using voice, gesture, remote button, or on-screen control.
5. The app records shots without requiring manual interaction.
6. Live graphics show makes, attempts, percentage, streak, zone, and one
   prioritized coaching cue.
7. Optional audio or haptic feedback is delivered between shots.
8. The player pauses or ends the session using a gesture, voice command, or
   button.
9. The app generates a session summary and recommended next drill.

5.3 Recorded-video analysis

1. Import a video from the device or record inside the app.
2. Select the player when multiple players appear.
3. The app analyzes the video locally or uploads it with consent for advanced
   cloud analysis.
4. Detected shots and important moments appear on a timeline.
5. The player reviews slow-motion clips, synchronized metrics, and coaching
   recommendations.

5.4 Coach-managed training

1. Coach creates an athlete or team workspace.
2. Athlete accepts an invitation or guardian approves access.
3. Coach assigns drills, shot targets, and due dates.
4. Athlete completes sessions on iOS or Android.
5. Coach sees validated results, confidence, video clips, and progress.
6. Coach adds timestamped visual or voice feedback.
7. Athlete receives the next training assignment.

7. COMPUTER-VISION AND MACHINE-LEARNING ARCHITECTURE
===============================================================================

7.1 Live inference pipeline

1. Capture camera frame and timestamp.
2. Correct rotation and lens distortion.
3. Evaluate frame quality.
4. Run RTMDet-Tiny object detection at the selected adaptive interval.
5. Update ByteTrack object identities.
6. Run Kalman prediction between detector frames.
7. Crop the primary-player region.
8. Run MediaPipe Pose.
9. Run MediaPipe Hands when hands are visible and the shot state requires it.
10. Smooth landmarks using confidence-aware temporal filters.
11. Associate hands, player, ball, and hoop.
12. Update the shot-state machine.
13. Calculate eligible metrics.
14. Update the overlay renderer.
15. Store compact session events and selected video frames.

7.2 Adaptive inference

- Use device capability, thermal state, battery level, resolution, and scene
  complexity to choose the inference schedule.
- Do not run every model on every frame when it is unnecessary.
- Increase ball tracking frequency during gather, release, and flight.
- Reduce pose frequency while the player is retrieving the ball.
- Use regions of interest to reduce computation.
- Degrade graphics before degrading critical shot counting.
- Display a clear message when device limitations affect accuracy.

7.3 Pose engines

Primary mobile engine:

- MediaPipe Pose.
- MediaPipe Hands.
- Optimized for on-device performance.
- Used for single-player live sessions and detailed hand landmarks.

Advanced engine:

- RTMPose or RTMPose WholeBody.
- Fine-tuned using basketball movement and occlusion data.
- Used for multiple players, imported video, difficult viewpoints, and premium
  analysis.
- Exported to an approved mobile or server inference format.

7.4 Object-detection engine

- RTMDet-Tiny for supported live mobile devices.
- Larger RTMDet model for cloud or high-performance offline analysis.
- Custom basketball taxonomy.
- Multi-scale training.
- Motion blur, exposure, compression, occlusion, and background augmentation.
- Hard-negative examples including heads, signs, lights, clocks, and circular
  objects that resemble a basketball.

7.5 Tracking

- ByteTrack for identity association.
- Kalman state including center, size, velocity, and acceleration estimates.
- Optional optical flow during fast ball flight.
- Trajectory-history buffer with timestamps.
- Re-identification logic when a player or ball temporarily disappears.
- Track termination and duplicate prevention rules.

7.6 Geometry and calibration

- Camera intrinsic estimation from device profiles where possible.
- Perspective transform from detected or manually selected court points.
- Known rim diameter, rim height, ball diameter, and court dimensions used only
  when calibration confidence is sufficient.
- Pixel-to-court coordinate mapping.
- Camera-angle eligibility rules for each metric.
- Uncertainty propagation from object and landmark confidence.
- No false precision: values must use ranges or confidence bands when required.

7.7 Shot-event engine

The shot-event engine must combine:

- Ball location, velocity, and acceleration.
- Ball-to-hand distance.
- Wrist, elbow, shoulder, hip, knee, and ankle motion.
- Player-to-hoop orientation.
- Ball ascent and descent.
- Rim-plane crossing.
- Net movement when visible.
- Backboard and rim contacts.
- Temporal continuity.

The engine must distinguish:

- Shot.
- Pass.
- Dribble.
- Rebound.
- Ball pickup.
- Pump fake.
- Layup.
- Dunk.
- Tip-in.
- Blocked attempt.
- Ball entering from another court.

7.8 Confidence framework

Every event and measurement must include:

- Raw model confidence.
- Tracking continuity.
- Calibration confidence.
- Camera-angle eligibility.
- Occlusion status.
- Final reliability level: high, medium, low, or unavailable.

Low-confidence values must not:

- Trigger strong coaching claims.
- Enter verified leaderboards.
- Distort player trend reports.
- Be presented with misleading decimal precision.

7.9 Model update system

- Version every model and preprocessing pipeline.
- Record model version with each analyzed session.
- Support staged rollout and rollback.
- Compare new and previous models on locked validation sets.
- Prevent a model update from silently changing historical comparisons.
- Reprocess selected sessions only with user consent and clear labeling.

8.3 Dataset governance

- Obtain explicit consent for model-training use.
- Separate service-use consent from training-data consent.
- Provide an opt-out.
- Remove direct account identifiers from training media.
- Blur faces where identity is unnecessary.
- Apply stricter controls to data involving minors.
- Track dataset origin, license, consent, labeling version, and deletion status.
- Do not scrape private or copyrighted training footage without permission.
- Maintain a deletion propagation process for eligible requests.

8.4 Training workflow

1. Ingest consented and properly licensed videos.
2. Validate file integrity and metadata.
3. Sample frames and clips based on motion and uncertainty.
4. Pre-label with the current models.
5. Human reviewers correct annotations.
6. Run automated label-quality checks.
7. Split by player, location, and recording session to prevent leakage.
8. Train and tune models.
9. Evaluate on fixed and challenge datasets.
10. Conduct device benchmarks.
11. Run shadow testing.
12. Release through a staged model rollout.

8.5 Active learning

- Capture uncertain examples only with consent.
- Prioritize failure cases rather than random footage.
- Identify device, lighting, shot-type, and demographic coverage gaps.
- Use corrected makes and misses as quality signals.
- Require human validation before training.

9. TARGET MODEL AND PRODUCT QUALITY
===============================================================================

Targets are acceptance objectives, not public guarantees. They must be measured
on representative locked test sets.

9.1 Detection targets

- Ball detection mAP50 target: at least 0.90 on representative validation data.
- Rim detection mAP50 target: at least 0.95.
- Primary-player detection mAP50 target: at least 0.95.
- Report mAP50-95 in addition to mAP50.
- Maintain dedicated small-ball and motion-blur test subsets.

9.2 Tracking targets

- Shot-event ball-track continuity target: at least 95% on supported camera
  setups.
- Identity switches must be measured for multi-player scenes.
- Temporary occlusions must not create duplicate attempts.

9.3 Shot-recognition targets

- Attempt-count accuracy target: at least 98% in supported single-player
  conditions.
- Make/miss F1 target: at least 95% in controlled supported conditions.
- Make/miss F1 target: at least 90% across diverse real-world challenge sets.
- Uncertain outcomes must be surfaced instead of forced into a wrong class.

9.4 Biomechanics targets

- Joint accuracy measured using PCK and pixel error.
- Angle error measured against manually reviewed or higher-grade reference
  capture.
- Temporal release-point error target defined in frames and milliseconds.
- Calibration-dependent measurements validated against known court dimensions.
- Each metric must have its own approved validation threshold.

9.5 Performance targets

- Supported-device live experience: minimum 30 FPS camera preview.
- AI updates: target 15 to 30 processed frames per second depending on device.
- Overlay response target: under 100 milliseconds perceived latency.
- Shot-result display target: within 750 milliseconds after sufficient visual
  confirmation.
- Cold launch target: under 3 seconds on supported devices.
- Crash-free session target: at least 99.8%.
- No unbounded memory growth during a 90-minute workout.

- MediaCodec for supported video processing.
- Google Play Billing.
- Credential Manager and Google sign-in.
- WorkManager for reliable background synchronization.

10.4 Flutter-to-native boundary

- Use platform channels for low-frequency control and configuration.
- Use FFI or native textures for performance-critical frame and overlay paths.
- Do not copy full-resolution camera frames repeatedly through Dart.
- Keep camera capture and live inference on native worker threads.
- Send compact events and landmarks to Flutter.
- Use a texture-based preview and synchronized overlay timestamps.

10.5 Overlay rendering

- Use Flutter CustomPainter, Impeller-compatible rendering, native GPU layers,
  or a validated shader pipeline.
- Draw body skeleton, hand landmarks, ball boxes, hoop boxes, trajectory, arc,
  angles, shot zones, and counters.
- Use temporal interpolation to prevent jumping graphics.
- Scale correctly across resolutions and orientations.
- Keep overlays optional for clean recording.
- Do not purchase static overlay graphics to imitate live analysis; live
  graphics must be generated from model output.

22. RISKS AND MITIGATIONS
===============================================================================

Risk: The basketball is small and moves quickly.
Mitigation: Multi-scale training, high-shutter camera guidance, adaptive
inference, Kalman prediction, optical flow, and basketball-specific data.

Risk: A single camera cannot provide perfect 3D measurements.
Mitigation: Camera-angle eligibility, court calibration, uncertainty ranges,
multi-angle recorded analysis, and no false precision.

Risk: Pose landmarks become unstable during occlusion.
Mitigation: Player ROI tracking, confidence-aware smoothing, temporal pose
models, RTMPose upgrade, and hiding invalid metrics.

Risk: Different gyms create false detections.
Mitigation: Diverse hard-negative data, scene-quality checks, saved court
profiles, and continuous challenge-set testing.

Risk: Live AI drains battery or overheats devices.
Mitigation: Adaptive inference, native acceleration, ROI processing, model
quantization, resolution scaling, and thermal-aware feature degradation.

Risk: Incorrect coaching damages trust.
Mitigation: Deterministic metric validation, confidence gates, qualified coach
review, limited claims, and correction feedback.

Risk: Youth video creates privacy and safety exposure.
Mitigation: Local-first processing, guardian controls, private defaults, strict
retention, no open messaging, consent management, and legal review.

Risk: Model licensing creates commercial obligations.
Mitigation: Use MediaPipe, RTMDet/MMDetection, RTMPose/MMPose, ByteTrack, and
OpenCV only after dependency-level license review. Do not introduce Ultralytics
YOLO into the proprietary production product without an approved commercial
license and legal review.

Risk: Product tries to ship too many metrics before validation.
Mitigation: Release only metrics that meet defined quality gates. Keep
experimental metrics labeled and outside authoritative coaching.
